Your Church Survey · November 2026

Our Strength

Inspiring worship service

— “This is a place we believe is worth coming to—and worth bringing others to.”

Many of us come ready to participate, and we can see something in our worship gatherings that matters. There is a sense that what happens here can connect with real life and help us turn toward God. Perhaps most encouragingly, we believe these gatherings can also be a place where people who are not yet part of church life might come and find something meaningful.

That does not mean every gathering reaches every one of us in the same way. Some of us do not always look forward to coming, and there are times when worship can leave us less engaged than we would hope. But underneath that tension is something we value: gathering together still has purpose for us, and we want to be part of it rather than simply watch from the edges.

Thank you for giving us a shared place where we can come, participate, encounter God and welcome others into something we ourselves value.

Our Struggle

Effective structures

— “We want to contribute, but we don’t always know how all our efforts fit together.”

There is willingness and ability among us. Many of us know something of what God has given us, can see value in what we contribute, and experience God using it. Yet it can be difficult to see where we are going together or how the different parts of our church connect. Planning, coordination and shared responsibility do not always give us the clarity and support we need to offer what we have with confidence.

That can leave a gap between wanting to contribute and experiencing ourselves as part of a genuinely shared endeavour. We do not simply want more things to do. We want to know that what each of us brings belongs within something we understand and can participate in together.

Please help us experience church life as something we share—not just through the tasks we each perform, but through clearer purpose, stronger connection and the sense that we really are carrying it together.

What draws us near

Inspiring worship service

— “Maybe I could come and see for myself.”

If we were wondering whether to come closer, it would matter that this appears to be a church where people beyond the existing community already find their way into worship. We might hope to encounter something more than a gathering designed only for those who already know how everything works: something connected with real life, where people come prepared to participate and where encountering God is genuinely possible.

We might not expect every part of the experience to connect immediately. But knowing that people already inside can readily say why they come—and that there seems to be an openness to people who are not yet part of the church—could make crossing the threshold feel less daunting. Thank you for making it imaginable that there could be something here for us too.

What gives us pause

Effective structures

— “If I came closer, would I eventually find where I fit?”

Getting through the door is one thing; finding a meaningful place within a community is another. If we stayed, or came back and wanted to become involved again, we might wonder how easily we could discover what we have to offer, understand where the church is going, and find our place within what everyone is doing together.

There are encouraging signs that people who are already contributing can experience God using what they bring. That makes the question more important, not less: would there be a clear way for someone moving in from the edges to grow from being welcomed into genuinely belonging and participating? Please make room for us not only to arrive, but eventually to discover how we might belong and contribute too.

What you can take heart in

“There is already life here that you do not have to manufacture.”

You are carrying responsibility among people who already bring significant life with them. There are people here who recognise their gifts, believe God uses what they contribute, deepen relationships beyond the church and participate actively when the church gathers. There is also a strong practical instinct to help people in need. This is not a body waiting passively for a few people to make church happen for them.

That matters for you. You do not have to be the source of all the energy, faithfulness or capacity the church needs. Indeed, part of what is already present can be received by you: other people have something from God to bring to this body. Their contribution can become part of what sustains you as well as what extends the church’s life.

Take heart in that. Your responsibility is exercised among people in whom God is already at work.

What needs to be carried

“Help the life that is already present become genuinely shared life.”

The more searching question is not whether people are willing to contribute, but whether the church is sufficiently able to hold those contributions together. People can often recognise what they bring and see value in it, yet the shared picture is much less clear: where the church is going, how its different parts fit together, how ministry is planned, how people are equipped, and how their contribution is reviewed and supported.

There is a particular stewardship issue here because collaboration itself appears fragile. The experience that leaders can prefer doing the work themselves rather than working with others sits alongside indications that leaders do not always remain concentrated on the work for which they are gifted. The survey cannot tell you why that pattern exists, nor does it establish that those carrying responsibility caused the wider constraint. But it does make the question difficult to avoid: is responsibility being carried in ways that help the capacity already present throughout the body become shared capacity?

That may require you to carry some things very deliberately. Clarity of purpose, genuine collaboration, coordination and the development of others do not simply emerge because willing people are present. Yet carrying responsibility for those conditions is different from carrying all the ministry yourself. Some things are yours to hold precisely so that other things no longer need to be.

There is freedom as well as challenge in that distinction. You can cultivate conditions in which people understand where they are going together, receive support for what they carry and increasingly contribute alongside one another. You cannot manufacture the life that results. You can plant and water faithfully, make room for the gifts God has already distributed through the body, and allow yourself to receive from them too. God gives the growth.

Our current Maximum Factor is Gift-based ministry, while Loving relationships is our Minimum Factor. Loving relationships was also our previous Minimum Factor, so the technical anchors have not changed places. But the story underneath them has changed considerably. Loving relationships has risen from 37.8 to 47.2—moving from well below the NCD mean to much nearer it—while the church as a whole has strengthened from an average of 49.7 to 57.2. Our strongest current capacity remains people finding meaningful ways to contribute; our greatest relative constraint remains the depth and accessibility of our relationships. What is different now is how much more relational and participatory life surrounds that constraint.

Maximum Factor — Gift-based ministry

“More of us are finding a meaningful place to contribute.”

Gift-based ministry is strong in absolute as well as relative terms. At 65.9, it sits just over one standard deviation above the NCD normative mean. This is not simply the best part of an otherwise constrained profile: there is substantial life here. People know their gifts (66.3), and the fit between those gifts and what they actually do has become especially strong (73.7). The latter has risen substantially from 59.7. More people also experience leaders removing barriers to their ministry (42.3 → 57.1), while accountability for commitments has strengthened markedly (42.0 → 56.0).

This suggests that our strength is not merely having willing volunteers. There is growing alignment between who people are, what they can bring and the space available for them to bring it. That capacity is reinforced by the very substantial rise in Empowering leadership (53.0 → 63.2). Even the negatively worded statement that leaders prefer doing the work themselves rather than collaborating moved from 54.7 to 63.2; because this item is negatively worded, that rise means less experience of leaders retaining the work themselves. The current picture therefore contains considerably more evidence of contribution being enabled and responsibility being shared.

Yet there is an important qualification. Feeling supported in ministry has actually weakened from 60.3 to 54.5, and experiencing the benefits of teamwork has fallen from 68.2 to 63.1. Both remain positive experiences, especially teamwork, so these declines should not be exaggerated. But together they suggest that finding a place to contribute is currently stronger than experiencing all the relational support around that contribution.

Minimum Factor — Loving relationships

“We are becoming more relational, but deeper mutual care is still uneven.”

Loving relationships remains our Minimum Factor, but it does not represent the same degree of constraint it did previously. Its movement from 37.8 to 47.2 is substantial. Previously it sat more than twelve points below the NCD mean; it is now only about three points below it. Calling it simply our continuing “weakness” would therefore miss one of the most important developments in the whole dataset.

Several experiences show what has changed. Telling someone when they have done a good job has risen from 36.8 to 56.4, while experiencing an atmosphere shaped by praise and compliments has risen from 37.5 to 56.2. Being able to tell other Christians about our feelings has moved from 36.8 to 51.5, and being able to rely on friends at church from 35.4 to 46.0. These are not isolated movements. Together they point toward a shared culture that has become appreciably more affirming, emotionally open and relationally supportive.

That development reaches beyond Loving relationships itself. Small-group help with life’s challenges has risen extraordinarily strongly, from 48.4 to 70.9, while belonging to a group where personal problems can be discussed has risen from 45.5 to 60.8. Knowing that other church members pray for us has risen from 42.7 to 55.5. Our previous principal constraint has therefore not merely changed numerically: people now report substantially more relational life around several different dimensions of church experience.

But the remaining constraint becomes clearer when we look at where that relational life is and is not readily available. The broad statement that it is possible in our church to talk with others about personal problems has barely moved, from 29.9 to 32.0, and remains exceptionally low. At the same time, the much more specific experience of having a group where personal problems can be discussed is now strong at 60.8. That contrast is especially revealing. Some of us appear to have found contexts of genuine relational depth without that depth yet becoming a readily accessible feature of church life as a whole.

There is another important tension at the threshold of belonging: “New Christians find friends in our church quickly” has moved from an already low 42.1 to 39.1. This decline is modest compared with the scale of relational improvement elsewhere, but its current level makes it consequential. The church has become substantially more affirming and relational for many people already within it, while quickly finding friendship remains difficult for those newly entering that relational world.

The developmental story, then, is not simply that Gift-based ministry is still highest and Loving relationships is still lowest. That unchanged ranking conceals considerable cultural movement. The relational reality that previously represented our greatest constraint has genuinely strengthened, alongside major growth in small-group support, empowering leadership, worship and outward concern. Our overall church experience is substantially healthier than before.

That changes what our present Minimum Factor means. We now have considerably more people experiencing affirmation, support, meaningful contribution and relationships in which deeper sharing is possible. Yet that life is unevenly distributed. We appear increasingly able to give people a meaningful place to contribute, and increasingly able to create pockets of deep support, while still finding it harder to make that depth of relationship broadly accessible—particularly when someone needs to bring personal difficulty into the wider community or is trying to find friendship as a newer person.

The significant story is therefore not that our previous constraint has simply persisted. Something that once constrained our shared life quite severely has genuinely become more alive. What this now makes visible is the difference between having relational life among us and that life being readily accessible across the whole body. Our growing capacity for contribution, encouragement and mutual support makes that remaining unevenness more—not less—important to understanding the kind of church we are experiencing now.

Our current Maximum Factor is Gift-based ministry. People increasingly know what they have to offer, find meaningful opportunities to use it, and experience greater freedom and responsibility in contributing to our shared life. Our Minimum Factor is Loving relationships, which was also our previous Minimum Factor. Yet its meaning has changed substantially: relationships remain our greatest relative constraint, but people now report considerably more affirmation, emotional openness, prayer and support than before. Particularly striking is the strength of support now experienced in some smaller groups, alongside the continuing difficulty of finding that same depth of openness across the wider church.

So the picture is not simply one strong area and one weak area. We have become a community with greater capacity both to contribute and, in important settings, to carry one another. The question Scripture brings to this developing reality is not merely how we can do more. It is what kind of body these growing capacities are forming us to become.

1 Corinthians 12:14–26 — Gifts belong to a body

Paul writes to a Corinthian church rich in spiritual gifts but troubled by the way people understood their place in relation to one another. His answer is not to diminish giftedness. Nor is it simply to organise everybody’s contribution more efficiently. He relocates gifts within the reality of a body whose members need one another.

For the body is not one member, but many. If the foot should say, “Because I am not a hand, I do not belong to the body,” it does not for that reason cease to belong to the body. And if the ear should say, “Because I am not an eye, I do not belong to the body,” it does not for that reason cease to belong to the body.

If the whole body were an eye, where would hearing be? If the whole were hearing, where would the sense of smell be? But now God has placed the members in the body, each one of them, just as he intended.

The eye cannot say to the hand, “I have no need of you,” nor again the head to the feet, “I have no need of you.” Instead, the members of the body that seem weaker are all the more necessary… so that there might be no division in the body, but that the members might have the same care for one another. And if one member suffers, all the members suffer together; if one member is honoured, all the members rejoice together.

There is much here to receive with gratitude. Increasingly, our people appear to know that they are a hand, an eye, an ear—that what God has placed within them has a meaningful place in the body. Finding a closer fit between people’s gifts and their contribution is genuine capacity within our present church life.

But Paul’s image presses beyond having a place to contribute. His climax is mutual dependence: “I have need of you.” The purpose of differentiated gifts is not merely that every member becomes useful, but that members become mutually necessary to one another. And the movement culminates in shared care, shared suffering and shared rejoicing.

That reframes the tension in our present experience. We are increasingly able to contribute, yet support around contribution is less uniformly strong. We have become considerably more affirming and relational, yet some people still struggle to find places where personal difficulty can be spoken openly. Some smaller communities now provide remarkable support, while that same accessibility to deeper relationship is much less evident across our wider life.

Paul therefore gives us a richer question than, “Is everyone using their gifts?” He invites us to ask, “Are our gifts drawing us into deeper mutual dependence?”

That includes receiving as much as contributing. “I need you” is as essential to Paul’s body as “I can serve you.” A healthy body is not a collection of increasingly capable people who each make their contribution. It is a people learning to give what they have received and to need what God has placed in someone else.

Ephesians 4:15–16 — Growth happens through connection

Paul develops a closely related image in Ephesians, but here his emphasis is maturity. The destination is Christ, and the whole body participates in its growth.

Speaking truth in love, let us grow in every way into him who is the head, Christ. From him the whole body, fitted and held together through every supporting connection, according to the working appropriate to each individual part, brings about the body’s growth, building itself up in love.

The Greek expression translated here as “every supporting connection” includes the word ἁφή (haphē), referring in this context to a connection or point of contact through which the body is held together and supplied. Paul’s picture is therefore not simply that every individual part functions well. The connections between the parts matter to the growth of the whole.

That nuance speaks unusually clearly into our present reality.

We have evidence of considerably more life in the individual parts: gifts being recognised and exercised, barriers to contribution being reduced, responsibility becoming more widely shared. We also have evidence that the connections themselves have strengthened. Appreciation is expressed more readily. People find it easier to speak about their feelings. Prayer and support are more widely experienced. Some groups have become places where people can bring the actual challenges of their lives.

This deserves gratitude. What was previously a much deeper relational constraint is not simply frozen in place. Our reported experience shows that shared culture can change.

Yet Ephesians prevents us from mistaking increased activity for the whole meaning of growth. Paul holds together “the working appropriate to each individual part” and the connections by which the whole body is “fitted and held together.” We need both.

That makes one contrast in our present experience especially revealing. Some of us now experience strong support in groups where personal problems can be discussed, while the wider experience of simply being able to talk with others in church about personal struggles remains deeply constrained. Newer Christians also appear to find friendship less readily than we might hope. There is relational life among us, but access to its deeper forms remains uneven.

Scripture does not turn that observation into a verdict. Instead, it enlarges our understanding of what the life already growing among us is for. The gifts of the parts and the connections between the parts belong to the same vision of maturity. Growing capacity to contribute creates greater possibilities for the body; growing capacity to know, need, support and receive one another enables that contribution to become genuinely shared life.

Perhaps that is the invitation contained within our present moment. We do not need to choose between gratitude for how far relational life has come and honesty about how much further it can deepen. The very growth we can now see gives us reason for hope about what remains unfinished.

And the centre of Paul’s vision is neither our technique nor our determination. The body grows “from him”—from Christ. We can recognise gifts, make room, strengthen connections, tell the truth in love and offer ourselves to one another. But the life being formed among us is received before it is achieved.

What has grown can therefore be received gratefully rather than claimed as an accomplishment. What remains constrained can be faced honestly without becoming a condemnation. And our increasing capacity to contribute can become something richer than greater participation: it can become a deeper experience of being Christ’s body, in which we learn both “I have something to give you” and “I have need of you.”

A Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank you for the life that is growing among us. Thank you for gifts being recognised, for people finding meaningful places to contribute, and for relationships in which encouragement, prayer, honesty and care are becoming more real.

Keep us humble enough to receive this life as a gift, and honest enough to see where our connections with one another are still thin or difficult to enter. Teach us not only to offer what you have given us, but to receive what you have placed in one another—to rejoice together, to carry difficulty together, and to make room for those still seeking a place among us.

Hold us together in you. Let every part bring what you have given, let every connection become capable of carrying your life, and grow us together into Christ in love. Amen.

How can I make a difference?

  • Three Ways to Add Life
  • The Vine: A Disciple Reality Check LBC

Meaningful church culture grows through ordinary interactions, and each of us can participate in the life that is already becoming more visible among us.

Name the good you see

Tell one person specifically what you appreciate about who they are or what they bring. Encouragement has become much more present among us; every sincere word of appreciation helps this growing culture become something we continue to give one another.

Let someone know you

In your next conversation with someone you trust, share one real thing about how life is going rather than stopping at the comfortable answer. Deeper openness is growing among us, but it is still much easier to find in some relationships and groups than across our whole community; you can make one relationship a little safer for honesty by going first.

Make room for someone else

Notice something another person could bring, and invite or encourage them to bring it—then resist the urge to do it for them. We are increasingly finding meaningful ways to use our gifts; receiving another person’s contribution helps turn that growing capacity into a church where we genuinely need and value what God has given each other.

How can I make a difference?

  • Three Ways to Add Life
  • The Vine: A Disciple Reality Check LBC

The Disciple Reality Check consists of three enduring questions that bring our shared journey back to our own discipleship. The questions beneath them arise from both where our church is now and what has changed since our previous survey, but everyone receives them in the same way: we answer about our own life, not someone else’s. At heart, they are questions about abiding in Christ—receiving his life and allowing that life to bear fruit through us.

1. Do you know what Jesus delights in when he looks at you?

  • Can you recognise and receive with gratitude the gifts, capacities and particular contribution Jesus has entrusted to you, without needing your usefulness to become the measure of your worth?
  • Where have you noticed greater freedom in yourself to encourage, appreciate, pray for or be more open with others—and can you receive that growing capacity as something good rather than immediately focusing on what is still unfinished?
  • Are you able to receive the care, gifts and support of others as readily as you offer your own contribution, trusting that needing other members of Christ’s body does not diminish what you bring?

2. Are you addressing what Jesus most wants to transform in your life?

  • When something personal is difficult, do you allow yourself to become genuinely known by trusted people, or is there a part of you that still prefers to remain useful, involved or friendly without becoming vulnerable?
  • When tension arises with another person, what happens in you: do you move towards honest, loving conversation, or avoid the discomfort while hoping the relationship will somehow resolve itself?
  • As encouragement and appreciation become more natural among us, is there anyone you still find difficult to affirm, forgive, receive or make relational room for—and what might Jesus want to transform in you there?

3. Are you helping someone else answer the other two questions?

  • Is there someone whose gifts or contribution you can see clearly—and have you helped them recognise what is good and life-giving in what Jesus has placed within them?
  • Are you becoming the kind of person with whom someone else can speak honestly about what is difficult, without needing to perform, appear strong or have everything resolved first?
  • When another person has something to offer, do you genuinely make room to receive it—even when allowing their contribution means you do less, relinquish control or discover that you need what they bring?

These questions can be especially fruitful when shared with another person or a small group. Answer them about yourself, listen to how others answer them about themselves, and resist using them to diagnose one another, our leaders or the church.

There is much more to a life with Jesus than this, but there is not less.
